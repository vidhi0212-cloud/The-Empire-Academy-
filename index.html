Subject 01 · 10 minutes

Real Estate — How property markets work

A real estate market is where buyers, sellers, landlords, tenants and developers exchange property or property rights. Prices are shaped by demand, supply, financing, infrastructure and expectations.

Core concepts

  • Demand: how many people want to buy or rent at different prices. Jobs, population growth and household formation can increase demand.
  • Supply: available homes, plots and commercial spaces. New construction, land constraints and approvals affect supply.
  • Primary market: property sold for the first time, usually by a developer.
  • Secondary market: resale of an existing property between owners.
  • Value drivers: location, connectivity, infrastructure, neighbourhood quality, building condition and legal title.

Rental yield

Gross rental yield = (Annual rent ÷ Property price) × 100
Example: ₹18,000 monthly rent × 12 = ₹2,16,000 annual rent. On a ₹36,00,000 property, gross yield = 6%.

Net yield is lower after maintenance, vacancy, property tax, insurance, brokerage and other costs. Consider loan interest and taxes separately.

Practical task: Pick two Ahmedabad localities. Compare connectivity, nearby jobs/education, infrastructure, asking prices and monthly rents. Calculate gross yield for one comparable property in each locality. Use actual listings and label estimates clearly.
Subject 02 · 10 minutes

Finance — Income, wealth and compounding

  • Income: money received during a period, such as salary, business profit or rent.
  • Expenses: money spent during a period.
  • Surplus: income minus expenses; available for saving, investing or debt repayment.
  • Assets: things you own with economic value.
  • Liabilities: amounts you owe.
Net worth = Total assets − Total liabilities
Monthly surplus = Take-home income − Monthly expenses

Income is a flow; net worth is a snapshot. Someone can earn a high income but build little wealth if spending and debt rise just as quickly.

Compounding

Compounding means returns can earn returns over time. Its effect depends on amount, rate, time and consistency. Returns are not guaranteed; market investments can fall in value.

Future value (lump sum) = P × (1 + r)n
P = starting amount, r = return per period, n = number of periods.
Practical task: Make a monthly money snapshot: income, fixed expenses, variable expenses, debt payments and surplus. Calculate net worth using realistic values. Do not share private account details.
Subject 03 · 10 minutes

Business & Entrepreneurship — The business engine

A business model explains how a company creates value for a customer, delivers it and earns money from it.

Common revenue models

  • One-time sale: customer pays once for a product or service.
  • Subscription: recurring payment for continuing access or service.
  • Commission: business earns a percentage or fee per transaction.
  • Marketplace: connects buyers and sellers and may charge listing or transaction fees.
  • Freemium: basic service is free; advanced features are paid.

Moat and scalability

A competitive moat is an advantage that makes it harder for competitors to take customers: trusted brand, network effects, cost advantage, proprietary technology, distribution or switching costs. Scalability means revenue can grow faster than operating costs.

Example: a property platform might earn through verified listings, tenant screening, rental-management subscriptions or transaction commissions. Each model has different customer-acquisition, trust and regulatory challenges.

Practical task: Choose a local business. Identify its customer, problem solved, revenue model, biggest cost and one possible moat. Ask: what would break if customer numbers doubled?
Subject 04 · 10 minutes

Psychology — Five biases that distort decisions

  • Confirmation bias: looking mainly for evidence that supports what you already believe.
  • Anchoring: relying too heavily on the first number or information you hear.
  • Loss aversion: feeling a loss more strongly than an equivalent gain.
  • Herd mentality: copying a crowd instead of independently checking evidence.
  • Overconfidence: overestimating the accuracy of your knowledge or predictions.

A simple decision routine

  1. Write down the decision and your objective.
  2. List the evidence supporting your choice.
  3. Actively search for evidence against it.
  4. Compare at least two alternatives, including doing nothing.
  5. Decide what new information would make you change your mind.
Practical task: Recall a recent purchase or investment decision. Identify whether an anchor, crowd opinion or fear of missing out influenced you. Write one check you could use next time.
Subject 05 · 10 minutes

Investment & Asset Management — Risk before return

An investment is made with the expectation of future benefit, but the outcome is uncertain. Higher expected returns generally come with higher risk; a higher promised return is not automatically a better investment.

Key risk types

  • Market risk: asset prices fall due to broad market movements.
  • Credit risk: a borrower or issuer fails to pay.
  • Liquidity risk: an asset cannot be sold quickly at a fair price.
  • Inflation risk: purchasing power falls as prices rise.
  • Interest-rate risk: changing rates affect borrowing costs and asset values.

Risk management basics

  • Diversify instead of depending on one asset, company or sector.
  • Match investments to time horizon and purpose.
  • Keep emergency money accessible; avoid investing money needed immediately.
  • Understand fees, taxes, lock-ins and exit conditions.
  • Check the downside scenario, not just the best-case return.

Real estate can involve concentration, vacancy, title, construction, maintenance and liquidity risks. A property may take months to sell and cannot always be divided into small portions.

Practical task: Choose one familiar asset (bank deposit, mutual fund or property). Write its expected benefit, three risks, how quickly you could access the money and what could cause a loss. This is an educational exercise, not a buy/sell recommendation.
Subject 06 · 10 minutes

Personal Development — Negotiation that creates options

Negotiation is a structured conversation in which people with partly shared and partly different interests try to reach an agreement.

  • BATNA: Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement — your strongest realistic option if no deal is reached.
  • Reservation point: the least favourable deal you are willing to accept, based on alternatives and priorities.
  • Active listening: focusing on the other person's meaning, asking clarifying questions and summarising accurately.
  • Objective criteria: market rates, comparable offers, written policies or measurable standards used to evaluate proposals.

Before a negotiation

  1. Define what you want and why it matters.
  2. Research a fair range using reliable comparisons.
  3. Identify your BATNA and walk-away point.
  4. List what you can trade that has low cost to you but value to the other side.
  5. Ask questions before making assumptions.
Practical task: Imagine negotiating the rent of a flat. Prepare your target rent, evidence from comparable listings, your BATNA, three questions for the landlord and two non-price terms you could negotiate (such as repairs or lease duration).
